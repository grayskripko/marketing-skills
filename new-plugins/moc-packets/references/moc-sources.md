Every URL below was opened on 2026-10-12. The package makes no runtime network requests; links are for human reading. Scope: US federal OSHA Process Safety Management (29 CFR 1910.119) and EPA Risk Management Program Program 3 prevention (40 CFR part 68, subpart D). State plans, other countries and site procedures can add requirements. Regulation text was read on osha.gov and in the eCFR (current as of 2026-10-08 for titles 29 and 40).

## 1. 29 CFR 1910.119 — Process safety management of highly hazardous chemicals

https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/regulations/standardnumber/1910/1910.119 — read 2026-10-12. Current online regulation. eCFR copy: https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-29/subtitle-B/chapter-XVII/part-1910/subpart-H/section-1910.119 — read 2026-10-12.

- (b) "Replacement in kind means a replacement which satisfies the design specification." "Facility means the buildings, containers or equipment which contain a process."
- (l)(1) Written procedures to manage changes (except for "replacements in kind") to process chemicals, technology, equipment, and procedures; and changes to facilities that affect a covered process.
- (l)(2) The procedures assure that these are addressed **prior to any change**: (i) technical basis for the proposed change; (ii) impact of change on safety and health; (iii) modifications to operating procedures; (iv) necessary time period for the change; (v) authorization requirements for the proposed change.
- (l)(3) Employees involved in operating the process, and maintenance and contract employees whose job tasks will be affected, are informed of and trained in the change **prior to start-up** of the process or affected part.
- (l)(4) If the change changes process safety information under (d), that information is updated accordingly. (l)(5) If it changes operating procedures or practices under (f), those are updated accordingly.
- (d)(1) Hazard information: toxicity, permissible exposure limits, physical, reactivity, corrosivity, thermal and chemical stability data, hazardous effects of foreseeable inadvertent mixing. SDSs may be used to the extent they contain this.
- (d)(2)(i) Technology: block flow or simplified process flow diagram; process chemistry; maximum intended inventory; safe upper and lower limits (temperatures, pressures, flows, compositions); evaluation of consequences of deviations.
- (d)(3)(i) Equipment: materials of construction; P&IDs; electrical classification; relief system design and design basis; ventilation system design; design codes and standards employed; material and energy balances (processes built after May 26, 1992); safety systems (interlocks, detection or suppression). (d)(3)(ii) document compliance with recognized and generally accepted good engineering practices (RAGAGEP). (d)(3)(iii) equipment built to codes no longer in general use: determine and document it is safe.
- (f)(1) Operating procedures cover initial startup, normal operations, temporary operations, emergency shutdown, emergency operations, normal shutdown, startup after turnaround or emergency shutdown; operating limits with consequences of deviation and steps to correct; safety and health considerations; safety systems and their functions. (f)(3) Procedures reviewed as often as necessary to reflect changes; annual certification that they are current and accurate. (f)(4) Safe work practices (lockout/tagout, confined space, opening process equipment or piping, entrance control).
- (g)(1)–(3) Initial and refresher training (at least every three years); training record holds employee identity, date of training and means used to verify understanding.
- (h) Contractor employers train contract employees in work practices and document it.
- (i)(1) Pre-startup safety review (PSSR) for new facilities and for modified facilities **when the modification is significant enough to require a change in the process safety information**. (i)(2) Before highly hazardous chemicals are introduced, the PSSR confirms: (i) construction and equipment in accordance with design specifications; (ii) safety, operating, maintenance and emergency procedures in place and adequate; (iii) new facilities: PHA performed and recommendations resolved or implemented; modified facilities meet (l); (iv) training of each employee involved in operating the process completed.
- (j)(1) Mechanical integrity equipment: pressure vessels and storage tanks; piping systems including valves; relief and vent systems and devices; emergency shutdown systems; controls (monitoring devices, sensors, alarms, interlocks); pumps. (j)(4) inspection and test frequency per manufacturers' recommendations and good engineering practice. (j)(6)(ii)–(iii) installation checked against design specifications and manufacturer's instructions; spare parts suitable for the process application.
- (e)(6) PHA updated and revalidated at least every five years. (o)(1) compliance audit at least every three years.
- Citation line on the page: 57 FR 23060, June 1, 1992; 61 FR 9227, March 7, 1996; 77 FR 17776, March 26, 2012; 78 FR 9313, Feb. 8, 2013.

## 2. Appendix C to 1910.119 — Compliance guidelines (nonmandatory), section 11 "Managing Change" and section 8 "Pre-Startup Safety"

Same URL as section 1 (Appendix C follows the standard on that page) — read 2026-10-12. Nonmandatory: it "neither adds nor detracts from the requirements of the standard."

- Change includes all modifications to equipment, procedures, raw materials and processing conditions other than replacement in kind. Operation outside the established operating parameters requires review and approval by a written MOC procedure.
- Technology changes can come from production rate, raw materials, experimentation, equipment unavailability, new equipment, new product development, catalyst change and operating condition changes. Equipment changes include materials of construction, equipment specifications, piping arrangements, experimental equipment, computer program revisions and changes in alarms and interlocks.
- Temporary changes are subject to MOC. A time limit for temporary changes should be established and monitored; MOC is used to return equipment and procedures to their original or designed condition at the end of the temporary change.
- A typical change form may include: description and purpose; technical basis; safety and health considerations; documentation of changes for operating procedures, maintenance procedures, inspection and testing, P&IDs, electrical classification; training and communications; pre-startup inspection; duration if temporary; approvals and authorization.
- Minor, well-understood changes: a checklist reviewed by an authorized person may suffice; complex or significant design changes: a hazard evaluation with approvals by operations, maintenance and safety may be appropriate.
- Changes to P&IDs, raw materials, procedures, mechanical integrity programs and electrical classification are noted so the revisions are made permanent when drawings and manuals are updated; copies are kept accessible to operators and PHA teams.
- Section 8: changes other than replacement in kind made during a shutdown or turnaround go through MOC; P&IDs and procedures updated; refresher or additional training where changes affect it; incident, audit and PHA recommendations reviewed before startup.

## 3. OSHA directive CPL 02-01-065, Process Safety Management of Highly Hazardous Chemicals

https://www.osha.gov/sites/default/files/enforcement/directives/CPL_02-01-065.pdf — read 2026-10-12. Signature date December 14, 2023; effective January 26, 2024. Landing page: https://www.osha.gov/enforcement/directives/cpl-02-01-065 — read 2026-10-12. Enforcement policy, not a rule.

- QL-01: MOCs for equipment and chemicals are retained for the life of the covered process through their incorporation in the PSI under (l)(4).
- QL-02: MOCs for procedures and practices may be retained only until incorporated into the next PHA revalidation or update under (e)(6).
- QL-03: OSHA does not require an "MOC log", but recommends a tracking process, especially with many MOCs in different stages.
- QL-04: Replacing a gasket that satisfies the original gasket's design specifications is replacement in kind; a gasket of different material, composition, shape, size or design needs an MOC.
- QL-05: No MOC is required to prove replacement in kind, but (d)(3) PSI must cover all component parts, original or replacement, and stay accurate for the life of the process.
- QL-06 / QL-07: MOC applies to changes in maintenance procedures and to changes in inspection, testing and preventive maintenance frequencies; those changes are also captured in the written MI procedures under (j)(2).
- QL-08: (l)(3) training is not refresher training; it applies when changes affect the process.
- QL-09: Organizational changes that affect PSM at plant level trigger MOC: mergers, acquisitions, reorganizations; staffing levels or staff experience; contracting out that directly affects covered processes; budget cuts that affect covered processes. Corporate or administrative changes unrelated to operations or maintenance do not.
- QI-01: A spare pump piped in parallel with an existing pump is a modified facility; the new piping changes the P&IDs, so a PSSR is required and (l) applies.
- QI-02: A modified facility does not need a new PHA, but (l)(2)(ii) safety and health impact must be evaluated.
- QI-06: PSSR confirms equipment matches design specifications; (j)(6) quality assurance for new equipment can be included in the PSSR.
- QD-02: PSI is kept current for the life of the process and updated whenever changes other than replacement in kind are made.
- QE-03 (restart after extended shutdown): verify changes since the last PHA went through documented MOC; update P&IDs and procedures; evaluate training needs; review incident and audit recommendations; perform PSSR before introducing highly hazardous chemicals into modified facilities.

## 4. OSHA letters of interpretation used by the directive

- https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/standardinterpretations/2006-07-12-0 — read 2026-10-12. Palmer letter, July 12, 2006: MOC retention for life of the process via PSI; no MOC log required; replacement-in-kind needs no MOC, but (d)(3) PSI still covers the replacement part.
- https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/standardinterpretations/1996-10-31 — read 2026-10-12. Hazzan letter, October 31, 1996: MOC applies to changes in maintenance procedures and in test and inspection frequencies.
- https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/standardinterpretations/1996-01-11 — read 2026-10-12. Tolley letter, January 11, 1996: new versus modified facility; a facility connected to or dependent on an existing one is a modified facility; modifications other than replacement in kind are subject to MOC.
- https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/standardinterpretations/2009-03-31-0 — read 2026-10-12. Memorandum "Management of Organizational Change", March 31, 2009: organizational, staffing and budget changes that affect covered processes need MOC; citation is considered when a change proceeds without written MOC or when an MOC identified a hazard and actions were not taken in a timely way.

- https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-40/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-68/subpart-G/section-68.190 — § 68.190(b)(5)–(7): update the RMP within six months of a change that requires a revised PHA or hazard review, a revised offsite consequence analysis, or that alters the Program level.

## 6. US Chemical Safety Board — Management of Change safety bulletin

- https://www.csb.gov/management-of-change/ — read 2026-10-12. Safety Bulletin, August 28, 2001 (No. 2001-04-SB).
- https://www.csb.gov/csb-safety-bulletin-says-managing-change-is-essential-to-safe-chemical-process-operations/ — read 2026-10-12. Release of the same date.
- Lessons used: MOC covers operational deviations and variances from normal operation, not only preplanned technology, process and equipment changes; neither incident was an emergency and time was available for structured analysis; authorization level matched to risk; written safe limits; training for new procedures; periodic audit of the MOC system.

## 7. US Chemical Safety Board — Williams Geismar Olefins case study

- https://www.csb.gov/williams-olefins-plant-explosion-and-fire-/ — read 2026-10-12. Investigation page; final case study released October 19, 2016.
- https://www.csb.gov/assets/1/6/Williams_Case_Study_2016-10-19.pdf — read 2026-10-12.
- Errors used as checks: the PSSR for new reboiler block valves was dated February 1, 2001, the MOC was initiated March 2, 2001 and approved April 6, 2001, after the valves were in service; the MOC checklist focused on documentation and maintenance items, not on overpressure of the isolated reboiler; the P&ID update was left to a later PHA instead of being required by the MOC; a car seal recommended by a 2006 PHA was installed without an MOC or PSSR; MOC reviews passed desk to desk instead of a multidisciplinary review. Recommendations called for MOC and PSSR effectiveness metrics and independent assessments at least every three years.
