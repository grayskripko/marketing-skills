# The three layers

Use positioning, message order and wording as diagnostic lenses. Resolve positioning before a rewrite when it is a dependency; inspect all relevant layers and prioritise factual harm, decision blockage and likely impact. The L1–L3 labels are for you; in the answer, say "positioning", "message order" or "wording".

## L1 Positioning

Questions:
- Who exactly is this for? Can a reader tell within the first screen whether it is meant for them?
- What does it replace? The reader's current alternative: a spreadsheet, a person, another tool, doing nothing.
- Which category does the reader put it in? If the page never says, the reader guesses.
- Which substantiated differences matter to the intended audience? On campaign pages, look for a clear primary reason to choose; on broader pages, assess relevant value and routing across the page.

Signs L1 is broken:
- the audience is described as "businesses" or "teams" with no qualifier;
- the headline could sit on a competitor's page unchanged;
- the page lists features before saying what problem they solve;
- intended audiences cannot identify a clear route or relevant value. Multiple clearly labelled audience routes are appropriate on routing pages.

Use positioning decisions already supplied. Where materially different audiences or offers remain plausible, propose grounded options and explain the uncertainty. Ask for a choice only if it blocks the requested work; make an authorised recommendation without presenting speculation as established positioning.

## L2 Message priority

Questions:
- How many jobs does the first screen try to do (explain, sell, impress, collect a lead)? One dominant decision is a useful campaign default; routing and comparison pages may support clearly separated decisions.
- Do the sections follow the order of the reader's questions: what is it, is it for me, how does it work, can I trust it, what does it cost, what do I do next?
- Do the headings alone carry the argument (skim test)?
- Is there one primary call to action, or several competing ones?

Signs L2 is broken:
- three or more calls to action in the first screen with equal weight;
- proof is unavailable where the reader needs it to evaluate a material claim;
- an explanation needed for understanding or credibility is missing or arrives too late for that decision;
- headings fail to support scanning. Conventional navigation and comparison labels are acceptable. Order price, mechanism and proof by the reader’s decision and existing knowledge.

## L3 Wording

Questions:
- Is each sentence specific (a number, a named step, a named system) or vague?
- Is the reader the subject, or the company?
- How many stock phrases per 100 words (`stock-phrasing.md`)?
- Does each promise come with a mechanism sentence?

Signs L3 is broken while L1 and L2 hold: the argument is right and in the right order, but sentences are vague, company-centred or padded.

## Verdict wording

- "Fix positioning first: the page does not say who it is for, and the headline would fit any finance tool."
- "Positioning is clear; fix the message order first: the first screen asks for three different actions."
- "Positioning and order hold; the problem is wording: about 4 stock phrases per 100 words, and the main promise never says how it works."
