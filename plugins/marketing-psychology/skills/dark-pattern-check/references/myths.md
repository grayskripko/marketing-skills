# Common claims and short answers

Answer in one or two sentences, name the study (authors, year) from the ledger row, then offer the full evidence check. The Ledger column is for lookup and is not printed.

| Claim | Short answer | Ledger |
|---|---|---|
| Losses always hurt twice as much as gains | The average lab estimate is near 2, but it falls to about 1 in symmetric, unordered designs; treat "twice" as design-dependent | L04 |
| People need seven touches before they buy | No study for this in the ledger; folklore | L34 |
| Open loops work because people remember unfinished tasks | A 2025 meta-analysis found no memory advantage; the tendency to resume interrupted tasks does hold | L22, L23 |
| Fewer options always convert better | Meta-analyses find an average effect near zero, with overload only under specific conditions | L15 |
| A decoy tier always pushes buyers to the target plan | Lab effect with numeric tables; often absent in realistic settings | L14 |
| Prices ending in 9 always win | Not always: the left-digit effect needs the leftmost digit to change; separately, $9 endings raised demand in three catalogue field tests even against a lower price with the same leading digit, but less when the item also carried a "Sale" cue; endings can signal a discount, which may not suit a premium tier | L06 |
| Scarcity always raises perceived value | Small lab effects; only legitimate when the limit is real | L16 |
| Nudges lift conversion by 30% or more | At scale the average was 1.4 points (8.0% relative); journal averages are inflated by selective publication | L21 |
| A/B test won once, so the principle is proven | One result on one page is not a general rule; many published findings fail to replicate | L26 |
| Tired users buy on impulse late in the funnel | The ego-depletion effect did not replicate in a 23-lab study | L24 |
