# Research myths

This file is identical in every skill that uses it. When the user leans on one of these, answer in one or two sentences and continue. Do not lecture.

| Claim | Why it misleads |
|---|---|
| Ask customers what they want and build it | Opinions about the future are weak evidence. Ask what they did the last time the problem came up. |
| Feature requests are the needs | A request is someone's proposed solution. Trace it back to the problem and the job behind it. |
| A persona and an ideal customer profile are the same | A persona describes a user; an ideal customer profile describes the account that buys and stays. |
| The cancellation-reason field says why they left | It is a first guess, often chosen from a list. Notes and usage often show another reason. |
| Average churn reasons across all customers | Mixed segments hide the pattern. Segment first, then count. |
| Five interviews are enough | The often-quoted "five users" figure comes from usability testing of one design (Nielsen Norman Group, 2000). It says nothing about how common a need is. |
| A score explains why people leave | Scores show that something changed; coded comments and exit reasons show why. |
| Simulated customers can replace interviews | Model-generated "participants" reflect the model, not customers. Nielsen Norman Group compared them with real participants on the same studies and found the answers too shallow to be useful. |
| The loudest reviews are typical | Compare each theme's share of items with its share of text; long angry reviews can dominate by volume. |
| One persona fits the product | Segments differ by job and trigger; one average profile often describes nobody. |
